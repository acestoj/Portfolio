Sheet 02 / 06 Control systems Year: 2026 Tools: MATLAB · Simulink

Chilled-Water Cooling Loop: Controller Design, Tuning and Validation

Objective: Design a single fixed-gain controller for a chilled-water cooling loop that holds two very different duties without retuning between them: pull the outlet temperature down 1 °C on command inside 120 s with under 10% overshoot, and reject a sustained loss of cooling capacity while keeping the temperature within 0.03 °C of nominal.

This was my individual scope within a university team project designing a hydrogen refuelling plant: one of four sub-systems, each taken by a different member, with the sub-systems meeting at shared interfaces. My part was the cooling loop and the fan that drives it. The plant parameters and the two test scenarios were set by the brief, so the engineering here is the controller, not the facility: a second-order linearised model, a fan with 600 rpm of authority either side of its operating point, and a specification that proportional control cannot meet.

Stability Analysis

  • Block-diagram reduction to the closed-loop and disturbance transfer functions
  • Root locus solved by hand for the breakaway at kp = 8.15, confirmed in MATLAB at 8.17
  • Routh array showed stability for every positive gain, so no ultimate gain exists

Tuning Progression

  • P, then PI, then PID, each stage triggered by a measured failure of the last
  • 1960-design grid search across the PI gain space, evaluated on both scenarios
  • Closed-loop Ziegler–Nichols ruled out, open-loop reaction curve used instead

Actuator Reality

  • Fan saturation modelled, exposing integrator wind-up the linear design hid
  • Derivative moved onto the measurement to remove derivative kick
  • Setpoint weighting swept, then held against sensor lag and a 1.8× plant-gain spread

Simulink model: two-degree-of-freedom PID built from discrete gain, integrator and filtered-derivative blocks, with reverse action, fan saturation and the disturbance entering at the plant input

Proportional control at three gains: each one meets a different requirement and fails the others

PI control removes the steady-state error but cannot meet both 120 s limits, because the pole sum is fixed

Ziegler–Nichols PID: the fan sits at its limit for about 18 s while the integrator keeps accumulating, giving 66.4% overshoot

From linear design to realisable controller: the ideal design demands 3593 rpm against a 1200 rpm limit, and each fix below brings it back inside

Final controller on both scenarios, same gains, no retuning

1 / 6

Result: A two-degree-of-freedom PID, kp = 527 rpm/°C, ki = 11.07, kd = 4956, derivative filter 2 s, setpoint weight 0.6. It meets both scenarios on one set of gains: zero overshoot and 68.8 s settling on the setpoint change, 98.0 s recovery and no steady-state deviation under the disturbance, with the fan peaking at 922 rpm against a 1200 rpm ceiling. Getting there took nine designs, and the useful part of the work is why each of the first eight failed.

Design 5 is the one worth dwelling on. On paper it was the best controller in the set: 8.4% overshoot, 75.2 s settling, every requirement met. It also asked the fan for 3593 rpm, three times what the hardware can deliver, almost all of it derivative kick acting on the instant the setpoint steps. Nothing in the linear simulation showed that, because a linear model has no limits in it. Putting the saturation block back in turned the best design into one that overshot 15.4%, and fixing it properly meant changing the controller structure rather than the gains: derivative onto the measurement, then setpoint weighting to undo the sluggishness that introduced. Designs 5 through 9 are all the same gains. Only the structure moved.

Next steps: The final design was checked against the slower of two candidate temperature sensors and a 1.8× spread in plant gain, and it held, but only with the faster sensor specified, so that sensor is a requirement rather than a preference. Beyond that, the model is linearised about one operating point and the cooling capacity is represented as a single lag, so the next step would be re-deriving it away from nominal and adding explicit anti-wind-up rather than relying on the controller never saturating.

Sheet 03 / 06 Control systems Year: 2026 Tools: MATLAB · Simulink · QUARC

Rotary Servo Position Control: Identification, PD Design and Ramp Tracking

Objective: Take a rotary servo from an unknown plant to a controller that tracks a moving reference accurately. Identify the motor model experimentally, close a position loop, design PD gains to a stated overshoot specification, then quantify and remove the tracking error that remains when the reference never stops moving.

The rig is a Quanser QUBE-Servo: a 24 V direct-drive motor with an inertia disc and a 2048 count/rev optical encoder, driven from Simulink in external mode through QUARC at a 2 ms fixed step. The same rotary chain appears in a camera gimbal axis, so I framed the work against that application: amplifier, torque constant, inertia, rate feedback, position integration and encoder feedback all have direct equivalents.

System Identification

  • Bump test on the open-loop plant to fit a first-order velocity model
  • Gain from Δωss/ΔVm; time constant at 63.2% of final value
  • Identified K = 23.206 rad/s/V and τ = 0.0923 s

Controller Design

  • Integrator added for position, giving K/[s(τs+1)]
  • Second-order form matched to get ωn and ζ from kp and kd
  • Derivative taken on the measurement, not the error, to avoid derivative kick
  • Filtered differentiator 100s/(s+100) to stop encoder noise being amplified

Tracking Analysis

  • Error transfer function derived and the Final Value Theorem applied
  • PD ramp error predicted as R0(1+Kkd)/Kkp
  • Integral action tested at ki = 0, 5 and 15 to lift the system from Type 1 to Type 2

Proportional gain sweep: measured overshoot and peak time against the second-order prediction

Three gain sets against the 2.54% overshoot specification: the design equations produced no overshoot at all

Closed-loop poles for the supplied, designed and final gains, over the locus traced as kd varies

Ramp tracking error: PD holds a constant offset, integral action drives it into the one-count encoder band

1 / 4

Result: The PD design equations did not survive contact with the hardware. Gains calculated for a 2.54% overshoot specification produced a response with no overshoot at all, because the derivative filter that keeps encoder noise out of the loop also changes the dynamics the design equations assumed. Retuning with the filter in place did the same thing. The gains that actually met the specification were found empirically at kp = 2.5 and kd = 0.065, giving 2.88% overshoot against the 2.54% target. The steady-state work went the other way: the derived ramp error predicted 0.2931 rad and the rig measured 0.2782 rad, a 5.1% agreement that held up the whole model chain.

That third row is the interesting one. The identified plant said 0.2155 V was needed to hold 5 rad/s; the logged command averaged 0.1405 V, a 34.8% shortfall that looks at first like a controller fault. Substituting the measured tracking error back into the control law returns 0.1410 V, within half a millivolt of what was logged. The controller was doing exactly what its equation said. The discrepancy sits in the plant model: the effective motor gain at 0.14 V is not the gain identified during a step test that ran at a much higher operating point, which deadband compensation and friction would both explain.

Next steps: Identify the plant across the operating range rather than at one point, so the model stays usable at the low voltages where the controller actually works. Include the derivative filter in the design equations instead of adding it afterwards, since it moved the response far enough to make the analytical gains useless. Push ki until wind-up and saturation appear, because the tested range was nowhere near the limit and five seconds was not long enough to call ki = 15 an optimum.

Sheet 04 / 06 Mechanism design Year: 2026 Tools: MATLAB · Simscape · Fusion 360

Jansen Leg Linkage: Synthesis, Analysis and Prototype

Objective: Synthesise a single-degree-of-freedom walking linkage to a specified gait: 86 mm step length at 0.05 m/s body speed, driven by one crank at 15 rpm, with six legs phased so a foot is always in contact with the ground.

The attraction of the Jansen linkage is that one crank drives the whole leg through pin joints only, with no sliders, so several legs can share a crankshaft cheaply. I verified the design five independent ways: hand calculation, MATLAB vector-loop solution, Simscape multibody simulation, Fusion 360 assembly, and a physical foamboard prototype built from 1:1 printed templates.

Mobility & Synthesis

  • Gruebler's equation: DoF = 3(8−1) − 2(10) = 1
  • Grashof check on both four-bar loops confirmed full 360° crank rotation
  • Foot path scaled to the 86 mm step length specification

Kinematic Analysis

  • Five link groups solved in sequence by Norton's K-method
  • Position, velocity and acceleration resolved at every pin
  • Transmission angles checked across stance and swing

Verification

  • Fusion assembly agreed with MATLAB to within 0.5 mm at every pin
  • Simscape matched the analytical foot path, velocity and acceleration
  • Physical prototype built and measured against predicted gait

Result: The analysis chain held together: Fusion, Simscape and the hand calculations all agreed, and the prototype walked with no stiff points, with lift-off to swing occurring where the model predicted. The physical gait itself came up short of target, and the gap between a model that agrees with itself and hardware that matches the model is the part worth reporting honestly.

Next steps: The crank speed and gait shape carried over well, but step length and body speed both fell roughly a quarter short. Foam board at 5 mm with pierced split-pin holes gives real joint clearance and link flexure, so the effective link lengths are not what the template specified. The next build would use a rigid material with reamed pin holes and bushes, and measure the foot path directly against the predicted curve rather than inferring it from body speed.

Sheet 05 / 06 Client: City of Stonnington Year: 2025 Tools: SPACE GASS

Cyclist & Pedestrian Bridge, City of Stonnington

Objective: Design and verify the preliminary structural system for a cable-stayed cyclist and pedestrian bridge for the City of Stonnington. The scope covered modelling the bridge in SPACE GASS, performing independent hand calculations, selecting steel members, designing the footing and validating all software outputs.

Structural Analysis

  • Created a 3D SPACE GASS model of the bridge
  • Applied dead, live and wind loading combinations
  • Determined governing member forces and support reactions

Verification

  • Checked bending, shear and axial capacities by hand
  • Verified footing bearing, sliding and overturning stability
  • Compared hand calculations against software outputs

Design Iteration

  • Updated footing after allowable bearing pressure reduced to 100 kPa
  • Re-checked stability and eccentricity
  • Identified main girder overstress requiring further optimisation

Member selection covered a 900WB257 welded beam for the main girder, 310UB40.4 crossbeams and a 219.1×6.4 mm CHS backstay, all in Grade 300/C350 steel.

Next steps: The main girder passed bending and shear but didn't satisfy the biaxial stress check under combined wind and gravity loading, or the lateral deflection limit under wind. The next step would be to model the full lateral restraint system, the A-frame, stay cables and bracing, rather than treating the girder as resisting wind alone. If the interaction check still didn't pass, the section would need to be revised for greater lateral stiffness, alongside fatigue assessment over the design life.

Warman Design & Build Competition: Seed Collection & Deposit Machine

Objective: Design and build an autonomous seed collection mechanism for the 2024 Weir Warman "Project FAMINE" competition. The robot was required to autonomously collect and deposit seed pods within a 400 mm cube and complete the task in under 120 seconds. My primary responsibility was the drivetrain and arm mechanism, including CAD design, Arduino control logic, mechanical integration and system testing.

Design specifications

Competition Constraints

  • 400 × 400 × 400 mm starting volume
  • Autonomous operation
  • Single activation
  • 120-second time limit

Hardware

  • Arduino Uno
  • DC gearmotor
  • Servo motor
  • Spur gear drivetrain
  • Laser-cut plywood chassis
  • 3D printed components

Software

  • Arduino IDE
  • Fusion 360

Key engineering decisions

Drivetrain

Designed a spur gear transmission to transfer motor torque directly to the steel drive shaft while maintaining compact packaging within the competition volume.

Arm Mechanism

Implemented servo-controlled positioning with predefined pickup and deposit angles to improve repeatability across different seed heights.

Structural Design

Designed the frame to remain within the competition volume while providing sufficient stiffness for arm operation without adding unnecessary mass.

Integration

Balanced packaging constraints between electronics, drivetrain and collection arm during assembly to ensure all subsystems could operate without interference.
